There is a word in Japanese aesthetics — _ma_ (間) — that does not translate cleanly into English. It is usually rendered as "negative space," but that phrase is misleading. Negative implies a subtraction, something taken away from a whole. _Ma_ is the opposite: it is the interval that makes the parts legible as parts.

A room with no gaps between the furniture is not a room. It is storage.

## Space is a unit of time

The most useful thing I know about layout is that vertical space is read as duration. A reader moving down a page experiences a large margin the way a listener experiences a rest in music — as a beat where nothing happens, and where the thing that just happened is allowed to settle.

This has a practical consequence. If two elements belong to the same thought, the space between them should be smaller than the space around the pair. That is the entire rule. Everything else in typographic hierarchy is an elaboration of it.

When designers complain that a page "feels cramped," they are almost never reacting to the size of the type. They are reacting to a page where every gap is the same, so nothing is grouped, so every element arrives with equal urgency.

## Three ratios worth memorising

Most of the spacing decisions on a reading page collapse into three relationships:

1. **Line to line.** Roughly 1.5 to 1.7 times the type size for body text. Tighter and the eye loses the return sweep; looser and lines stop reading as a paragraph.
2. **Paragraph to paragraph.** Either an indent or a blank line, never both. A blank line of about three quarters of the line height is usually enough.
3. **Section to section.** At least three times the paragraph gap. This is the one people get wrong. A heading that sits closer to the text above it than the text below it will attach itself to the wrong section, and no amount of bold weight will fix that.

> The heading belongs to what follows, not to what came before. Space it accordingly.

## Margins are not wasted

The hardest space to defend in a design review is the outer margin, because it looks like unused screen. Someone will always suggest widening the column to "use the space better."

Resist it. The measure — the length of a line of text — is a functional constraint, not an aesthetic preference. Somewhere between 45 and 80 characters, the eye can find the start of the next line reliably. Past that, it starts landing on the wrong line, and the reader begins to skim without deciding to.

A wide margin is not empty. It is doing the work of keeping the measure honest.

## Restraint compounds

The reason a page of ink and paper feels calm is not that it uses few colours. It is that every decision it makes is made once and then repeated. One type family. One accent. One spacing scale. One rule weight.

Variation is expensive. Every new size, weight, or colour you introduce has to earn its place by carrying meaning that nothing else was carrying. Most of them do not. They were added because a particular element looked slightly wrong in isolation, and the fix was applied locally rather than to the system.

Design the interval, and the elements mostly arrange themselves.
